# The 1961 Goldsboro B-52 Nuclear Accident

## Overview

On **January 24, 1961**, a U.S. Air Force B-52G Stratofortress (tail number 58-0187) broke apart in mid-air near **Goldsboro, North Carolina**, dropping two live **Mark 39 Mod 2 thermonuclear bombs** — each with a yield of **3.8 megatons** (roughly 250x the Hiroshima bomb). It remains one of the most famous "Broken Arrow" nuclear accidents of the Cold War, and declassified documents later revealed just how close the United States came to an accidental nuclear detonation on its own soil.

## Cold War Context: Operation "Cover All"

The B-52 was based at **Seymour Johnson Air Force Base** in Goldsboro and was flying as part of the Strategic Air Command's airborne alert program called **"Cover All"** (a predecessor to Operation Chrome Dome). The program kept nuclear-armed bombers airborne 24/7, flying staggered "ladder" routes into the Canadian Arctic and back. The logic: a bomber already in the air with live weapons couldn't be destroyed on the ground by a surprise Soviet first strike.

The downside, which safety engineers at Sandia National Laboratories had already flagged, was that flying fully-assembled, sealed-pit thermonuclear weapons around the clock was "fundamentally a matter of playing percentages." Absolute safety was impossible.

## The Accident

### The Fuel Leak
- Around **midnight on January 23–24, 1961**, the B-52 rendezvoused with a tanker for aerial refueling.
- The tanker crew spotted a **fuel leak in the B-52's right wing** and advised the aircraft commander, **Major Walter Scott Tulloch**.
- Refueling was aborted. Ground control directed the bomber to a holding pattern off the coast to burn off fuel.
- The leak rapidly worsened — **37,000 pounds of fuel lost in three minutes**. The aircraft was ordered to return immediately to Seymour Johnson AFB.

### Loss of Control
- Descending through **10,000 feet** on approach, the crew could no longer keep the aircraft in stable descent. The B-52 went into what was described as "a gyration best described as a spin" — the right wing had essentially failed structurally.
- At **9,000 feet**, the pilot ordered the crew to abandon ship.
- **Five crewmen** ejected or bailed out and landed safely. **One** ejected but died on landing. **Two** died in the crash. (3 fatalities, 5 survivors.)
- Notably, third pilot **Lt. Adam Mattocks** is the only person known to have successfully bailed out of the top hatch of a B-52 without an ejection seat.

### Breakup and Impact
- The pilotless aircraft broke up shortly after the crew bailed. Witnesses saw two flashes of red light, suggesting fuel explosions.
- At **12:35 a.m. EST on January 24**, the wreckage slammed into a **two-square-mile area of tobacco and cotton farmland** at Faro, in Nahunta Township, Wayne County — about **12 miles north of Goldsboro**.
- The two Mark 39 bombs separated from the aircraft somewhere between the crew ejecting and impact.

## The Two Bombs

### Weapon No. 1 (Serial No. 434909) — The One That Nearly Went Off

- Ejected around 9,000 feet. The twisting motion of the breakup pulled its safing pins out longitudinally — mimicking an intentional drop sequence.
- The arming rods withdrew, actuating the **MC-845 Bisch generator**, which kicked off the firing sequence: low-voltage thermal batteries activated, the **MC-543 timer** started its 42-second countdown, and the parachute deployed as designed.
- The barometric pressure switch operated normally and passed current to the **MC-772 Arm/Safe Switch**.
- **That switch was found in the "Safe" position** — the only thing that prevented a full nuclear detonation.
- The timer ran its full course, the high-voltage thermal battery initiated, and the nose crush-switch closed on impact. Under normal circumstances, the weapon would have fired. But because the Arm/Safe Switch hadn't activated the MC-788 High-Voltage Safing Switch, the X-Unit never charged. No detonation.
- The bomb was found **upright, largely intact**, parachute snagged in nearby trees, about a mile behind the main wreckage. Only damage: a broken nose plate, buried about 18 inches in the soil. About a pint of JP-4 jet fuel was found inside the bomb casing.
- EOD teams from Wright-Patterson AFB disassembled and safed it by disconnecting the tritium reservoir.

### Weapon No. 2 (Serial No. 359943) — The One That Buried Itself

- Separated from the aircraft later, between **5,000 and 2,000 feet** altitude.
- Its safing pins were also pulled and arming rods withdrawn, starting the firing sequence. But the parachute **failed to deploy** despite the deployment mechanism activating.
- The bomb hit the ground at high speed (free-fall), leaving a crater **5 feet deep and 9 feet in diameter**. The timer had only run 12–15 seconds — not enough for the high-voltage battery to activate.
- The impact was so violent that the **MC-772 Arm/Safe Switch's indicator drum rotated to "Arm"** — but post-mortem analysis at Sandia showed the switch had disconnected from its contacts and was never electrically armed. The impact damage itself prevented the circuit from closing.
- Lt. Jack ReVelle, the EOD officer on scene, famously recalled: *"Until my death I will never forget hearing my sergeant say, 'Lieutenant, we found the arm/safe switch.' And I said, 'Great.' He said, 'Not great. It's on arm.'"*
- The bomb broke into pieces on impact. The **primary stage** was recovered on January 30 at a depth of about 20 feet. Its high explosives had not detonated.
- Excavation continued to **42 feet**, hampered by freezing cold, a high water table, and deep mud. The effort was abandoned. The **thermonuclear secondary stage was never recovered** — it remains buried in the field, estimated at **180 ± 10 feet deep** (per UNC Chapel Hill analysis). The Air Force purchased an easement on the land; it still cannot be dug up or developed.

## How Close Did We Come?

This is where it gets sobering. The declassified record:

- **Parker F. Jones**, Sandia supervisor, wrote in a 1969 reassessment: *"One simple, dynamo-technology, low voltage switch stood between the United States and a major catastrophe."* He judged it **"credible"** that an electrical short during a mid-air breakup could have flipped the Arm/Safe Switch to "Arm" — in which case the Mk 39 Mod 2 **would have produced a nuclear blast**.
- **R.N. Brodie**, in a 1987 Sandia safety history, noted that these ready/safe switches — which needed only a 28-volt DC signal to operate — had been observed **many times** to inadvertently switch to "arm" from stray current. He concluded flatly: *"If [Weapon No. 1] in the Goldsboro accident had experienced inadvertent operation of its ready-safe switch prior to breakup of the aircraft, a nuclear detonation would have resulted."*
- Sandia engineer **Charlie Burks**: *"Unfortunately, there have been thirty-some incidents where the ready/safe switch was operated inadvertently. We're fortunate that the weapons involved at Goldsboro were not suffering from that same malady."*
- A 1969 Sandia analysis concluded the Mk 39 Mod 2 **"did not possess adequate safety for the airborne alert role in the B-52."**
- A safety modification (**Alt 197**) that would have added a better Arm/Safe Switch (the MC-1288) had been approved in January 1960 — a full year before the accident — but **had not been installed on either of the Goldsboro bombs**.

## What a Detonation Would Have Meant

A 3.8-megaton surface burst at Faro, NC would have:
- Vaporized everything within roughly a mile of ground zero
- Produced lethal blast effects across Goldsboro and Seymour Johnson AFB
- Sent lethal fallout down the East Coast depending on winds — potentially toward densely populated areas including Raleigh, Richmond, and Washington, D.C.
- Killed an estimated tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands

## Aftermath and Legacy

- The accident was kept largely quiet at the time. The full details — including Jones's "one switch" assessment — only became widely public in **2013** when journalist Eric Schlosser obtained declassified documents via FOIA for his book *Command and Control*, and the National Security Archive published additional Sandia reports in 2014.
- The Goldsboro accident, along with Palomares (Spain, 1966) and Thule (Greenland, 1968), drove major changes in U.S. nuclear weapons safety: the end of routine airborne alert flights with live weapons, stronger safety switches, insensitive high explosives, and eventually Permissive Action Links (PALs) as standard.
- The secondary stage of Weapon No. 2 is **still in the ground** in Wayne County. The site is marked, and the easement restricts excavation.
- A historical marker commemorating the crash was dedicated in Eureka, NC in 2012.
